about

Press a button, pick a film, take the picture.

The look is applied inside the camera, while the photo is being made. What lands on the card is the finished JPEG, no app or editing afterwards.

What this is.

EXCCD is an add-on for CHDK, the free community extension for old Canon compacts. It puts film-inspired looks on cameras with early CCD sensors: cameras that are now close to 20 years old, but still have a character newer sensors don't.

The looks work on the raw sensor data: tone curves, channel mixes tuned against reference shots, and Canon's own colour settings. On top come a few things newer cameras do, like Auto-ISO with a minimum shutter speed or a white balance shift.

It started as an experiment on a single IXUS 60 and is being ported to more models step by step. Much of the code was written with AI as a coding partner.

None of this would exist without the CHDK developers and their many years of open-source work.

  1. Pick looks

    On this website you choose which looks you want on your camera. Your browser packs them into one ZIP file; nothing gets uploaded anywhere.

  2. Unpack to the SD card

    EXCCD lives on the SD card and starts from there. The camera's own software isn't touched: take the card out and it's a normal Canon again.

  3. Pick a look, shoot

    Each look sets Canon's own colour settings and bends the raw sensor data with a tone curve, before the camera turns it into a JPEG. That's why it behaves like a camera setting, not like a filter on top.

Give it a moment. These cameras run on old Canon DIGIC processors that were never meant to do this. After each shot they take a few seconds to think, a little longer with heavier looks and effects. A small bar on the screen shows how far along it is, so you know the camera is busy and not stuck. The pause slows you down a bit, which suits this kind of camera. Just don't expect a burst mode any time soon ;)

Public beta

Work in progress, and that's fine.

EXCCD is a hobby project, and it isn't finished. It is used daily on an IXUS 60, but every camera model is a bit different inside: another sensor, another colour matrix (the maths that turns sensor values into colours), another firmware. So whenever real photos from a camera come in, the looks get re-tuned, and things will keep changing between versions.

  • Tested – IXUS 60 / SD600. Used day to day; the newest features are still being checked.
  • In testing – IXUS 132 / ELPH 115 IS and PowerShot SX110 IS. Built and partly tested on real cameras.
  • Ported, not tested – IXUS 145 / ELPH 135. Built, but nobody has run it on a real camera yet.

You can only download for cameras that are ready for it. Some sample photos are simulated on a PC from other shots until real photos from that camera are available; those are marked “simulated”.

Free and open source

It's free.

There's nothing to pay, and there won't be. EXCCD is a hobby project with no commercial side.

The code is GPL v2, the same licence as CHDK, so anyone can read it, change it and pass it on. The looks themselves (curves, labels, film table) are CC BY-SA 4.0. Everything is on GitHub. Bug reports, ideas and help with bringing it to another camera are always welcome.

Keeping it simple

Still a point-and-shoot.

CHDK can do a lot, and you see that in its menus: hundreds of settings. EXCCD keeps only what you actually need when you're out shooting. You pick a film, the screen stays clean, and everything else waits in a short options menu.

  1. One button

    On the , A single key (Print or Playback on most models) opens the film selection, the arrow keys flip through the films, FUNC/SET picks one. That's all you need to know to get started.

  2. A clean screen

    Normally the screen shows just the battery and the name of the film, plus a short note when something special is on, like a recipe or Auto-ISO. When you half-press the shutter or open Canon's menus, it gets out of the way and comes back afterwards.

  3. Films, not parameters

    You pick a film by its label, a bit like grabbing a roll from the fridge. Contrast, colour and tone curve come with it, so you don't have to set them yourself.

  4. Canon underneath

    Canon's menus, modes and buttons work as before. Pick Off and you get the camera's normal colours.

White balance

Your colours, set on the camera.

On film, the colour of the light is part of the look: daylight film under a lamp turns warm, in the shade it turns blue. EXCCD lets you play with that in two layers.

  1. White balance

    First you set the camera's own white balance under MENU → Look: Auto, Daylight, Cloudy, Tungsten, Fluorescent or Custom. It's Canon's normal setting; you just find it in the same menu as the rest of the look.

  2. WB shift

    On top of that you nudge the colour on a grid, like the white balance shift some cameras offer: left and right go from cyan to red, up and down between blue and yellow, nine steps each way. A few steps towards red and yellow make a look warmer, towards blue it gets cooler.

  3. Works with every look

    The shift is worked into the tone curve of whichever look you've picked, so it doesn't slow the camera down. Only with the film set to Off it does nothing, because then there's no curve; the menu then shows “needs a look”. With the black-and-white looks the shift has no colour to show; it acts like a weak colour filter instead and changes how bright reds or blues come out.

  4. For the curious

    One step is roughly 3 % more or less red or blue in the raw data. It isn't calibrated yet, so take the numbers as a feel, not a measurement. You see the shift in the photo, not on the screen while you frame.

Recipes

Save it as a recipe.

Some cameras let you save a film look plus a handful of settings as a recipe, a look of your own that you can call up again. EXCCD does that too, and the white balance and WB shift are part of it.

  1. What's in a recipe

    The film, white balance, WB shift, Dynamic+, colour noise filter, B&W filter and all effects. In short: everything under MENU → Look.

  2. What isn't

    Shutter speed, focus, zoom, flash, ND filter, RAW and double exposure. Those depend on the moment, not on the look, so a recipe leaves them alone.

  3. Save and switch

    Set everything up, then Look → Save as recipe and pick one of four slots, C1 to C4. To switch, go to Recipe at the top of the menu and flip with left and right. If you change something afterwards, a * after the slot name tells you.

  4. Share them

    Recipes live in a plain text file on the card, CHDK/FILMS/RECIPES.TXT. On the PC you can rename them, write new ones or pass them on; the file explains its own format. Saving on the camera rewrites the file, so your own comments in it get lost.

RAW develop

Change your mind later.

A DNG is the raw sensor data, before the camera has decided anything about colour or contrast. If you save one next to each JPEG, you can develop the same photo again later, with another film, right on the camera. A bit like taking the negative back to the lab and asking for a different print.

  1. Shoot with DNG

    Turn on MENU → RAW → Save RAW. From then on every photo also lands on the card as a DNG, next to the JPEG.

  2. Pick photo and film

    Later, choose RAW → RAW develop, pick the DNG and the film you want, and effects if you like.

  3. Take one helper shot

    Press the shutter once, at anything. Canon's JPEG engine only runs after a shot, so the camera needs one to do the work. You get a new JPEG; the DNG stays untouched.

White balance, Dynamic+ and the date for the date stamp come from the original photo. The Exif data (date, exposure, orientation) comes from the helper shot, so hold the camera the same way and don't use digital zoom. Developing takes a few seconds, longer with heavy effects; a bar shows how far it is.

Also in there

A few extras.

All optional, and all in the options menu (MENU). The effects and look settings belong to the look and are saved in a recipe; the camera settings depend on the moment and stay as they are when you switch.

Effects · MENU → Look → Effects

  • Black Mist

    A soft glow around bright areas, like a diffusion filter in front of the lens. It takes the hard edge off digital sharpness.

    Off · 1/8 · 1/4

  • Halation

    A red-orange halo around bright lights. On film without an anti-halation layer, light bounces back from the film base and glows red around lamps and bright edges.

    Off · On

  • Vignette

    Darker corners, like on old or simple lenses.

    Off · Light · Strong

  • Light Leak

    A warm streak of light from the left or right edge, like on a film camera with a leaky back. Side and position change from shot to shot.

    Off · On

  • Date stamp

    Orange digits in the corner, like the date stamp on 90s film compacts: '26 10 31

    Off · On

  • Grain

    Film grain, added to the raw data. Unlike coloured digital noise it has one colour, comes in clumps and is strongest in the midtones, modelled on how film grain behaves. On Auto it follows the speed (ISO) of the film you picked.

    Off · Auto · Fine · Medium · Coarse

  • Film frame

    A thin black border around the picture, like a print from the lab. It covers the outer edge of the image, so leave a little room when you frame.

    Off · Thin

The effects work on the raw sensor data before the camera makes the JPEG, so grain or a light leak becomes part of the photo instead of being laid on top afterwards. Heavier ones like halation and grain give the camera a few extra seconds to think about it.

Look settings · MENU → Look

  • Dynamic+

    For bright scenes: the camera exposes a little shorter so the sky doesn't burn out, and the curve brings the shadows back up. Only when the camera picks the shutter speed: in Tv, M or long shutter mode, and with flash, your exposure stays as it is.

    Off · +1 · +2

  • B&W colour filter

    Works like a coloured filter on the lens in black-and-white photography: yellow, orange or red darken blue skies and make clouds stand out. Only for the black-and-white looks and Tintype; with other looks the menu shows “B&W only”.

    Off · Yellow · Orange · Red

  • Colour noise filter

    Small sensors make coloured blotches in dark areas. This smooths them out and leaves the fine grey noise alone, which looks more like grain.

    Off · Auto · Light · Strong

Camera · MENU

  • Auto-ISO with a shutter limit

    Canon's Auto ISO tends to keep the ISO low and the shutter slow, so indoor photos get blurry. Here you set the slowest time you accept, like the minimum shutter speed setting on many newer cameras. The camera raises the ISO only when it has to, up to a maximum you choose. On Auto the limit follows the zoom: about 1 / focal length, never slower than 1/30. It steps in when the camera's ISO is on Auto, you haven't set a shutter speed yourself and the flash doesn't fire. It works in Auto, P, Av and the everyday scene modes (portrait, indoor, kids & pets, landscape, macro), but not in modes that want long exposures, like fireworks, night or long shutter, and not while a script runs. If the camera already picked an ISO above your maximum, it keeps that ISO instead of slowing the shutter down. When Auto-ISO is set but can't step in, the status line shows “A-ISO -”.

    Off · Auto · 1/8 … 1/500 · max. ISO 200 … 1600

  • Zone focus

    Focus at a fixed distance instead of autofocus. Handy on the street: the camera fires right away instead of hunting for focus first.

    AF · 0.5 m … ∞

  • Shutter speed

    Set the exposure time yourself, for long night shots or to freeze movement.

    Auto · 16″ … 1/1000

  • ND filter

    Many of these cameras have a small grey filter inside that cuts the light, often instead of a real aperture. Here you swing it in or out yourself.

    Auto · In · Out

  • Flash power

    A fixed flash strength instead of automatic. Low is nice for a bit of fill light.

    Auto · Low · Mid · High

  • Raw + film log

    Saves a DNG next to each JPEG if you want one. FILMLOG.CSV lists every shot with the film you used.

    Off · DNG

  • Double exposure

    Two shots on one frame. The second one is mixed into the first in the raw data, much like exposing a negative twice.

    Off · On

Firmware version

How do I find my firmware version?

With the installer (Windows): start the EXCCD installer, choose your camera, insert a card formatted in the camera and click Check firmware…. It tells you what to do next.

By hand:

  1. Download ver.req and vers.req and copy both to the top level of the SD card. Older models read the first one, newer ones the second.
  2. Put the card in the camera (not locked) and switch it on in playback mode.
  3. Hold FUNC/SET and press DISP. The camera shows a line like Firmware Ver GM1.00D. The letter at the end is the one that counts.
  4. Delete both files from the card afterwards.
02

Looks

Every look comes with two Dynamic+ variants (DR+1, DR+2) for bright scenes; they're always included. The small graph on each card is the look's tone curve: the dashed line means “no change”, above it the picture gets brighter, below it darker. Film type and ISO on the labels say what a look is modelled on; they don't change the camera's ISO (only the grain on Auto goes by it). White balance, WB shift and the effects aren't chosen here: you set them on the camera while shooting, and save the combinations you like as recipes.

01

Install, step by step

Takes about ten minutes and works the same way on every supported camera. The camera's firmware isn't changed; everything happens on the SD card. A few details (card size, the button that opens the looks) depend on the model.

optional, it fills in the details for your model

You need

  • the camera, with a charged battery
  • an SD card
  • a card reader and a Windows PC (macOS and Linux: by hand)

  1. Check the firmware

    EXCCD has to know where things sit inside the camera's software, and that's different for every firmware version. So each download only runs on one firmware version; the needs . Not sure which one you have? The installer can check it (Check firmware…), or see how to find it by hand.

  2. Download your looks

    Choose your camera and firmware on the looks page, pick your looks and download the ZIP. Just leave it in your Downloads folder; the installer looks for it there.

  3. Format the card in the camera

    Use the camera's own Format menu, not the PC, so the card is set up the way the camera expects it. This erases the card, so copy your photos off first.

  4. Run the installer

    Installer for Windows

    Check that it picked your download, your camera and the right card, then click Install EXCCD on the card. It only lists removable drives, so it can't touch your other disks. It also makes the card bootable, which means the camera can start EXCCD from it. If the card doesn't suit the camera (too big, wrong format), it says so before writing anything.

    Windows warns about an unknown app because the installer isn't signed. Signing costs money every year, and this is a free hobby project; the code is open on GitHub. Click More info → Run anyway. It asks for administrator rights because it writes the card's boot sector.

  5. Lock the card

    Slide the card's write-protect switch to LOCK and put it in the camera. It sounds odd, but on these cameras that's the signal to start EXCCD from the card. Photos are still saved as usual.

  6. Switch on, pick a look

    Give the camera a few seconds after switching on. Then, in shooting mode, press the key that opens the looks: Print on most models, Playback on many others, Video, Help or Shortcut on a few (choose your camera above to see which), flip through the looks with the arrow keys and confirm with FUNC/SET. The look you're shooting with is shown at the bottom of the screen. MENU in the film selection opens the EXCCD options.

Without the installer (macOS, Linux)
  1. Format the card in the camera.
  2. Unpack the ZIP to the top level of the card, so that DISKBOOT.BIN and the CHDK folder sit right there, not inside another folder.
  3. Older models (up to about 2007, like the IXUS 60 or A570 IS; choose your camera above to be sure): Eject the card and put it in the camera unlocked. Switch on in playback mode and choose MENU → Firm Update. That starts EXCCD until you switch off. To make it start on its own, open the film selection, press MENU, run System → Make Card Bootable once, then lock the card.
  4. All other models: This camera only startsThey only start EXCCD from a bootable card, so the card has to be made bootable once, for example with the tools from CHDK's guide Prepare your SD card. Then lock the card.
02

If it doesn't work

The camera starts normally, EXCCD doesn't show up
  • Is the card locked? Without LOCK the camera ignores EXCCD.
  • Is the card too big? See You need above.
  • Was the card formatted in the camera and then installed with the installer (or made bootable)?
  • Does the firmware match? Other versions simply won't start.
  • Is it the right button? On the it's Print.Most models use Print, others Playback, Video, Help or Shortcut. It only works in shooting mode, a few seconds after switching on.
Windows won't run the installer

SmartScreen blocks unsigned programs at first: click More info → Run anyway. If your antivirus removes it, you can check the source code and the build log on GitHub, or install by hand.

The installer doesn't find my card

It only shows removable drives. Use a card reader, close Explorer windows that show the card and click Refresh. Some built-in laptop readers report cards as fixed disks; a cheap USB reader helps.

A look doesn't match the samples

That's the beta part. Light, white balance and the camera model all change how a look turns out, and the looks are still being tuned camera by camera. Please report it, ideally with the photo, the look and your camera.

How do I get the normal camera back?

Unlock the card or take it out. To remove EXCCD for good, format the card in the camera.

03

Questions

What is CHDK?

CHDK (Canon Hack Development Kit) is a free add-on for Canon compacts, made by a community of volunteers since 2007. It loads from the SD card when the camera starts and adds things Canon never built in, like raw files, manual controls and scripts. EXCCD is built on top of it: CHDK with the film looks added and a much shorter menu.

Can this damage my camera?

EXCCD runs from the SD card, like CHDK, and doesn't change the camera's firmware. Without the card, the camera behaves exactly as before. Still, it's third-party software, so use it at your own risk.

Do I lose anything compared to the normal camera?

Not much, but a few things. With a look on, the camera is busy for a few seconds after each shot, so there's no quick burst. The buttonThe key that opens the looks (Print or Playback on most models) opens the film selection instead of doing its usual job. EXCCD starts from a locked SD card, and on most models that card can be at most 2 GB. Pick Off in the film selection and the camera uses its normal colours again; Canon's menus and modes stay as they are.

How is this different from a filter app?

A filter app works on the finished JPEG, so it starts from a picture the camera has already processed and compressed. EXCCD steps in earlier: the tone curve goes onto the sensor's raw data, together with Canon's own colour settings, and then the camera makes the JPEG as usual. The look is in the photo from the start, and there's nothing to apply afterwards.

Why is the camera busy for a few seconds after each shot?

That's EXCCD at work. The look is calculated on the full raw image, millions of pixels, by a processor from the time these cameras were new. So it needs a few seconds, a little longer with heavier looks, effects or a DNG on a slow card. A bar on the screen shows the progress. Looks set to Off or Daylight skip most of the work.

Will my photos look like real film?

No, and they're not meant to. A real film look only comes from real film. The looks borrow colours, contrast and a few quirks from films and old processes, but the photo still comes from a small CCD sensor, with its own character. That character is what makes these cameras worth using, so EXCCD builds on it instead of hiding it.

Can I keep a raw file?

Yes. CHDK can save a raw file (DNG) next to each JPEG. That's the unprocessed sensor data, which you can develop later on a PC, or on the camera with RAW develop. Turn it on in the options (MENU → RAW).

Which look did I use for a photo?

EXCCD writes every shot with its look to CHDK/FILMLOG.CSV on the card, a list you can open in any spreadsheet.

How do I update, or add looks later?

Come back, pick a new set, download it and run the installer again with the card unlocked. It replaces the CHDK folder and keeps FILMLOG.CSV and your photos. By hand: delete the old CHDK folder first, then unpack the new ZIP.

Your recipes and settings are replaced too. If you want to keep your recipes, copy CHDK/FILMS/RECIPES.TXT off the card first and back afterwards.

Which languages are the menus in?

English. German is on the card too: MENU → System → Language... → german.lng. The film labels stay in English.

What about other cameras?

The plan is to bring EXCCD to more old Canon compacts: CCD models first, later maybe CMOS ones too. Every model has to be ported (finding where things live in its firmware) and then tested on a real camera, so it takes a while. If you have one, you can help.

Is it finished?

No, it's a public beta. It works, but looks get re-tuned and features change as photos from more cameras come in.

Is it open source?

Yes. The code is GPL v2 like CHDK, the looks (curves, labels, film table) are CC BY-SA 4.0. Everything is on GitHub.

04

Help out

EXCCD is meant to grow beyond a single IXUS. Every bit of feedback helps, recipes are welcome, and the project is open for more camera models.

  • Report a problem

    Something broken, odd colours, an idea? Open an issue on GitHub. Camera, firmware, EXCCD version and a photo (of the display, too) help a lot to figure out what's going on.

  • Test on your camera

    Most models have only been built, not tried on a real camera yet. The looks are tuned with real photos from real cameras. The camera test walks you through it, no experience needed, about 15 minutes.

  • Send a pull request

    Fixes, ports and new looks are welcome. The porting guide explains what a new camera needs.

    Photograph the test chart

    The chart is a set of colour squares on your screen. Photographing it shows whether the colours come out right on your camera.

    1. Open the chart

      Turn your screen brightness up and click the button below. The chart fills the screen; click or press Esc to close it again. On a phone or tablet, turn it sideways first.

    2. Set up the camera

      Shooting mode Auto, flash off. Stand so the chart fills most of the camera's display and hold the camera still.

    3. Take three photos

      One with no film look (the camera's normal colours), then one each with two looks of your choice.

    Now look at the photos on the camera's display and compare them with the chart:

    When you pick a look, do you see its coloured tab on the display?
    In the photo without a look: is the red square red and the blue square blue?
    Does the grey strip look grey, without a strong colour cast?
    Do the photos with looks look different from the one without?
    Did the camera run without freezing or switching off?
